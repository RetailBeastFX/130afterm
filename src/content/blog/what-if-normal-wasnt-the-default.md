**We've gotten so used to measuring people by how well they function in the world around them that we rarely stop to question whether the world itself is part of the problem.**

I came across a post suggesting an interesting experiment.

Take a neurotypical person and put them in an environment built around neurodivergent people. Have them socialize, communicate, work, and navigate everyday situations according to a completely different set of expectations.

Then have the people in that environment evaluate them.

Document their communication problems. Point out their social shortcomings. Measure how well they process information, pick up on social cues, and adjust to unfamiliar situations.

Basically, take the same approach that's commonly used to evaluate neurodivergent people and turn it around.

Interesting how different that sounds when the shoe is on the other foot.

### Who Decided What's Normal?

Think about how many things we consider basic social skills.

Making eye contact. Holding a conversation. Making small talk. Knowing when to speak, when to laugh, how to respond, and how to pick up on things that aren't being said directly.

We're taught to treat these things like universal measures of social competence. If somebody struggles with them, people might assume they're awkward, difficult to communicate with, or lacking in some area.

But how much of that is actual inability, and how much of it comes down to being unfamiliar or uncomfortable with certain social expectations?

Some people communicate better when they're allowed to be direct. Some need more time to process information. Some don't enjoy small talk or feel comfortable participating in social interactions that seem to revolve around unspoken rules.

That doesn't automatically mean they don't know how to communicate.

Sometimes people just have different ways of processing and expressing themselves. The problem is that we're so accustomed to one particular style that we rarely consider whether our expectations are part of the disconnect.

### Communication Goes Both Ways

There's a concept called the double empathy problem that puts this into perspective.

The idea, introduced by autistic researcher Damian Milton, is that communication difficulties between neurotypical and neurodivergent people can go both ways.

We're used to hearing about how neurodivergent people struggle to understand social cues, interpret emotions, or communicate effectively with neurotypical people.

But what about the other direction?

How often do we consider that neurotypical people might also misunderstand neurodivergent people?

If two people process information differently, express themselves differently, and interpret social situations differently, why does the responsibility to adapt usually fall on just one of them?

Research has actually found that people with similar neurotypes can communicate and exchange information effectively with one another. That suggests the difficulty isn't necessarily an inability to communicate. Sometimes it's the mismatch between two different ways of communicating.

That's an important distinction.

### We Confuse Conformity With Capability

This goes beyond social interaction, too. You can see the same thing happening in the workplace.

We have all these expectations surrounding what a productive, professional, functional adult is supposed to look like.

You should communicate a certain way. Work well in groups. Respond quickly. Handle interruptions. Participate in meetings. Take direction without making things difficult.

And if somebody struggles with those expectations, their overall ability can start coming into question.

But let's say somebody works exceptionally well independently but struggles in an environment where they're constantly interrupted and micromanaged.

Or somebody has plenty of thoughts, ideas, and opinions but struggles to articulate them when they're unexpectedly put on the spot.

Maybe somebody communicates much better through writing than spontaneous conversation.

Are those people incapable, or are we judging their capabilities under conditions that don't necessarily bring out their strengths?

There's a difference between measuring what somebody can do and measuring how well they can operate under conditions that work for everybody else.

Unfortunately, those two things get confused all the time.

### Maybe We Should Examine the Environment Too

I'm not suggesting that every personal difficulty is caused by society or that changing someone's surroundings will magically resolve everything.

People have genuine challenges, and some require support regardless of their environment.

But I do think we put a lot of responsibility on individuals to adapt to environments that rarely question their own expectations.

We talk about teaching neurodivergent people how to communicate, socialize, behave professionally, and fit into the workplace.

How often do we put that same energy into teaching neurotypical people how to communicate with them?

How often do employers question whether their workplace expectations actually make sense?

How often do we consider that somebody might be capable of much more than their current environment allows them to demonstrate?

Not every established expectation is unreasonable. Sometimes standards exist for good reasons. But that doesn't mean every social convention, communication preference, or workplace habit deserves to be treated like a universal requirement.

There's a difference between having standards and expecting everybody to function the same way.

### Different Doesn't Automatically Mean Deficient

The original post got me thinking about how much our definition of normal depends on who's establishing the standard.

Take somebody who's spent their entire life functioning comfortably in one environment and put them somewhere that operates by completely different rules. They're probably going to struggle with things they never had to think twice about before.

That doesn't suddenly make them unintelligent, incapable, or socially deficient.

It means the conditions changed.

So why is it so difficult to extend that same consideration to people who don't naturally fit into the environments we've already established?

Being able to navigate the dominant social culture doesn't automatically make somebody more intelligent, more capable, or better adjusted than somebody who struggles with it.

It means they've learned how to navigate that particular culture.

I think we should be a little more careful about treating familiarity as proof of superiority. Just because something comes naturally to the majority doesn't mean it's the only reasonable way to function.

Maybe the issue isn't always that certain people need to learn how to function in society.

Maybe we also need to question why society has such a narrow idea of what functioning is supposed to look like.

**Sometimes we're not measuring how capable somebody is. We're measuring how well they can operate under conditions that work for everybody else.**
