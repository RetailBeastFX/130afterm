Some people are so proud of how much bullshit they've learned to tolerate that they've mistaken everybody else's refusal to tolerate it for weakness.

I came across a conversation about people with ADHD and autism struggling to maintain a traditional 40-hour workweek. And naturally, the comments brought out the usual suspects.

Apparently, if you can't keep up with the same work schedule as everybody else, you're lazy. If you need accommodations, you're entitled. If you explain that your brain doesn't operate the same way as somebody else's, you're just making excuses.

And if somebody else manages to do it with the same diagnosis? Well, there goes your entire argument. Pack it up. Case closed.

That's the problem with some of these conversations. People aren't even trying to understand the circumstances. They're looking for somebody to judge.

## Your Suffering Isn't a Professional Qualification

One thing I've noticed is how some people treat their ability to endure discomfort like it's a qualification.

They'll tell you they worked 40 hours with ADHD. They raised kids, paid bills, dealt with difficult bosses, and pushed through whatever life threw at them.

Okay. Respectfully, congratulations.

You worked 40 hours with ADHD? Here's your participation trophy. Nobody said you couldn't.

But what exactly does your personal experience prove about everybody else?

ADHD isn't a personality template. Neither is autism. Two people can have the same diagnosis and have completely different experiences with it.

One person might manage a full-time schedule without much trouble. Another might struggle with executive functioning, sensory overload, time management, communication, or the constant pressure of having to appear normal around people all day.

And sometimes the job itself is the problem. The environment, the schedule, the expectations, the commute, the social demands. All of it adds up.

People love saying, "Well, I did it."

Good for you. But your ability to tolerate something doesn't mean everybody else should be expected to tolerate it the same way.

That's like telling somebody with a broken leg that walking is easy because you managed to do it yesterday.

## Some of Y'all Don't Want Employees. You Want People Who Make You Comfortable.

This is another part that gets overlooked.

Some employers will tell you they want reliable, capable workers. But then they'll put more weight on how somebody presents themselves than what that person can actually do.

Can you make eye contact? Can you keep a conversation going? Can you smile at the right time? Can you make the manager feel like you like them? Can you sit through eight hours of unnecessary social interaction without looking uncomfortable?

Apparently, those things can matter more than whether you actually know how to do the job.

Imagine hiring a mechanic and caring more about his eye contact than whether he knows what's wrong with your transmission.

Your car doesn't give a fuck about his personality. It wants to know why the check engine light is on.

And that's where the conversation gets ridiculous. Somebody can be perfectly capable of doing the work but still get treated like a bad employee because they don't perform the expected version of friendliness, confidence, or enthusiasm.

Not everybody communicates the same way. Not everybody processes information the same way. Not everybody is comfortable being watched, interrupted, rushed, or constantly evaluated.

That doesn't automatically make somebody unreliable.

There are legitimate performance standards. People still have responsibilities. Employers have a business to run. Nobody is saying the work doesn't matter.

But if your definition of a good employee depends on everybody having the same personality, social skills, energy level, and way of functioning, maybe you're not measuring job performance as much as you're measuring how comfortable people make you.

## Apparently, Needing Help Is a Moral Failure

Then there's the argument about public assistance.

"Is it fair for somebody else to work more hours or contribute more in taxes to support somebody who can't financially support themselves?"

That's a fair question to ask. Public resources aren't unlimited. Eligibility matters. Accountability matters. Nobody is saying every claim should automatically be accepted without review.

But there's a difference between discussing how a system should work and treating the people who need it like they're beneath you.

We all depend on systems and other people in ways we don't always acknowledge. Nobody is completely self-made. We use public roads, schools, infrastructure, emergency services, and programs that exist because people collectively contribute to them.

The point of having a safety net is that people don't all experience the same circumstances or have the same needs.

And let's be honest. A person needing support doesn't automatically mean they're trying to get out of contributing.

Some people genuinely cannot maintain the same level of work without serious consequences. Some can work, but need a different schedule or environment. Some need treatment, accommodations, or time to get back on their feet.

The consequences aren't abstract: a [2025 UK study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39844532) of 30,000 adults diagnosed with ADHD found they lose an average of around 7 to 9 years of life expectancy compared to matched controls.

The answer can't always be, "Well, figure it out. Everybody else did."

That's not a solution. That's just passing the problem to somebody who's already struggling.

## The Real Problem Is How We Measure People

I think a lot of this comes down to how deeply people attach human worth to productivity.

You work full-time? Responsible.

You make good money? Successful.

You pay your own bills? Independent.

You can handle pressure without complaining? Strong.

But if you struggle to maintain those things, suddenly people start questioning your character.

Now you're lazy. You're weak. You're making excuses. You're looking for handouts.

It's interesting how quickly a conversation about someone's limitations turns into a judgment about their character.

And some people really do believe they've earned the right to look down on others because they managed to survive circumstances that somebody else couldn't.

But surviving isn't the same as thriving.

Just because you figured out how to function in a system doesn't mean the system is working for everybody. And just because you learned to live with certain conditions doesn't mean everybody else should have to suffer through them to prove something to you.

You don't get extra human points for being miserable and keeping it to yourself.

## Being Functional Doesn't Make You Superior

I'm not saying everybody who struggles with work is automatically right. I'm not saying employers shouldn't have expectations. And I'm definitely not saying personal responsibility disappears because somebody has a diagnosis.

I'm saying there's a difference between holding people accountable and deciding that anyone who struggles must have some kind of character defect.

Some of y'all hear somebody explain a limitation and immediately start looking for a reason to discredit it.

You don't ask what they're dealing with. You don't consider whether the environment is part of the problem. You just compare their experience to yours and decide they're not trying hard enough.

And somehow, after all that, you convince yourself you're the reasonable one.

Maybe you are hardworking. Maybe you've overcome a lot. Maybe you really did push through things that weren't easy.

But that doesn't make you an authority on what everybody else should be able to endure.

Your suffering isn't a standard everybody else has to meet.

And your ability to function isn't proof that somebody else is inferior because they can't.

Some of y'all have confused being functional with being superior.

That's not strength. That's just pride with a work schedule.
