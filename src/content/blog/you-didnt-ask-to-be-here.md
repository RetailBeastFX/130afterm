*Parenthood, non-consensual existence, and the obligations we inherit.*

There's something interesting about how casually we talk about having children.

People will spend months deciding whether they're ready to buy a house, what car they can afford, whether they should change careers, or whether they can handle another monthly expense. But bringing an entirely new human being into existence? That's often treated like the natural next step in life.

Get married. Have kids. Buy a house. Start a family. That's the blueprint, apparently.

And if you question any of it too much, somebody will eventually remind you that children are a blessing, that life is beautiful, or that you'll understand when you have your own.

But I think we're a little too comfortable romanticizing reproduction without seriously considering what we're actually doing.

**You're making a decision for someone who doesn't get a say in the matter.**

That person doesn't get to choose their parents, their financial circumstances, their genetics, their environment, their country, or the historical period they're born into. They don't get to negotiate the terms of existence before being brought into it.

They just show up.

And from that point forward, they're responsible for figuring everything out.

### The responsibilities nobody asked for

Think about what we're actually bringing someone into.

You're born with needs. You need food, water, shelter, clothing, healthcare, and eventually some degree of financial independence. None of these things are optional just because you didn't ask to be here.

You grow up, go to school, learn how to behave, figure out what you're good at, and eventually enter an economy where your ability to access basic necessities is largely dependent on your ability to earn money.

You sell your time. You exchange your labor for a paycheck. You pay for the privilege of having somewhere to sleep, something to eat, and a little breathing room between obligations.

And if you're fortunate, you might eventually earn enough to enjoy some of the time you've spent working to afford.

That's the arrangement most people inherit.

We call it growing up. Becoming independent. Being responsible.

But strip away the familiar language for a second.

You didn't agree to participate in any of this. You were born into it, and now you're expected to make it work.

And that's before accounting for everything else that can make existence difficult.

You could spend your entire childhood feeling misunderstood. You could struggle socially, develop health problems, experience poverty, lose people you love, or spend years trying to find somewhere you feel like you belong.

Your parents can love you, provide for you, and do everything they reasonably can. They still can't guarantee that you'll enjoy being alive.

That's an uncomfortable reality of parenthood that doesn't get nearly enough consideration.

### Having children isn't an accomplishment in itself

One thing I've never particularly understood is the superiority complex some people develop around having children.

As though reproducing automatically makes someone more mature, fulfilled, responsible, or accomplished than someone who chooses not to.

Having a child doesn't automatically mean you're prepared to raise one. It doesn't mean you've figured out life. It doesn't mean you've developed some extraordinary understanding of the human experience.

And it certainly doesn't mean somebody without children is missing some fundamental component of being human.

Children aren't accessories. They aren't relationship milestones, retirement plans, miniature versions of yourself, or trophies to display your accomplishments.

They're people.

People who will eventually have their own opinions, disappointments, ambitions, insecurities, and complicated relationships with the world.

They aren't here to give your life meaning. They're going to have to figure out what gives their own lives meaning.

And that's a pretty significant responsibility to impose on another person.

### The problem with assuming everything will work out

I understand that nobody can predict the future.

Most parents aren't sitting around hoping their children will struggle. They want them to be happy, healthy, successful, and loved. They imagine birthdays, graduations, family vacations, grandchildren, and all the other experiences people associate with raising a family.

But good intentions aren't guarantees.

You can give a child a wonderful upbringing and still watch them struggle with things you never anticipated.

You can provide financial stability and still have a child who struggles with loneliness, depression, disability, or a sense of alienation.

You can teach them everything you know and still send them into a world that doesn't particularly care whether they're prepared.

And perhaps that's part of what bothers me about how casually we approach reproduction.

We're making a decision whose consequences could last an entire lifetime, based largely on our hopes for how that lifetime might turn out.

There's an argument that life is worth experiencing precisely because of its uncertainty. That even with hardship, there's beauty, love, discovery, humor, and opportunity.

I don't necessarily disagree.

But the possibility of a wonderful life doesn't erase the possibility of a miserable one.

Both are on the table.

### And then there's the expectation of gratitude

This might be the strangest part.

You're expected to be grateful for being alive, even though you never requested existence in the first place.

Questioning the circumstances of your birth can be interpreted as disrespect toward your parents. Expressing dissatisfaction with life can be treated as ingratitude. Acknowledging that existence is difficult can make people uncomfortable because it challenges the assumption that being alive is an unconditional gift.

But why should someone be obligated to view their existence as a gift simply because someone else wanted to give it to them?

Parents deserve appreciation for the care, sacrifices, and opportunities they provide. That's a separate conversation from whether the child owes them gratitude for the initial decision to create them.

Those things shouldn't be conflated.

You can appreciate your parents and still recognize that you never had a say in being born.

Both statements can coexist.

### I'm not saying nobody should have children

I don't think the answer is necessarily that nobody should reproduce.

People experience genuine fulfillment through parenthood. Families can provide extraordinary amounts of love, security, support, and companionship. Some people genuinely want to raise another human being and are prepared to make substantial sacrifices to do so.

I'm not dismissing that.

But I do think we should be more honest about what we're asking of the people we bring into existence.

Having children shouldn't be treated as an automatic milestone, an unquestionable good, or something people pursue simply because that's what adults are supposed to do.

And maybe we should spend less time questioning why somebody doesn't want children and more time questioning why we're so comfortable making that decision on behalf of somebody who cannot possibly consent.

Because the person being born doesn't get to choose whether they want the experience.

They inherit the whole thing.

The bills. The expectations. The responsibilities. The uncertainty. The good and the bad.

**You didn't ask to be here. But now that you're here, you're expected to figure it out.**

And I think that's worth thinking about before deciding to bring somebody else along.
